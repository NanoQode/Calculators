Once a subscription is accepted, most of what a mortgage investor knows about the investment arrives as documents: statements, notices, financial statements and tax slips. Mortgage investment reporting to investors is therefore not an administrative footnote. It is the main way you find out whether the loans behind your money are performing, whether distributions are holding up and whether your ability to redeem has changed.

This guide covers what each document is, who sends it and which gaps deserve a question, for holders of a mortgage investment corporation (MIC), a mortgage fund, or a direct or fractional mortgage. It is general education, not investment, tax or legal advice.

## What kind of reporting will I get as a mortgage investor?

The reporting you receive depends on the investment structure you hold, and it is set out in the offering memorandum (OM) or loan documents rather than by a single national standard. A MIC shareholder owns shares in a company and receives company-level reporting; a direct or syndicated investor holds an interest in specific loans and receives loan-level reporting.

Up to four parties may send you something: the **issuer** (statements, distribution notices, audited financial statements, tax slips); the **mortgage administrator**, which collects borrower payments into trust and usually reports to direct and syndicated investors; the **exempt market dealer (EMD)** that handled your subscription and know-your-client review; and, for registered plans, the **self-directed plan trustee**, which reports the plan's holdings.

A MIC that raises money only under prospectus exemptions is generally not a reporting issuer, so it does not file the public continuous-disclosure record a listed company files. Whether an instrument such as National Instrument 81-106 (which governs investment funds) applies depends on how the issuer is structured, and some ongoing disclosure may be required depending on the province and exemption used. Treat the OM as the reference point: it should say what the issuer will send, how often and by when.

## What documents should I receive as a mortgage investor?

The core set for a MIC shareholder is a record of the holding, distribution statements, annual audited financial statements, a tax slip and notices of material change; direct and syndicated investors should also hold the loan-level file. The table compares what each structure typically provides; confirm against your own documents.

| Document | MIC or fund shareholder | Direct or fractional mortgage investor | What to check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Offering memorandum and amendments | Received before subscribing and when amended | A disclosure document for the specific loan or offering | Risk factors, redemption terms, fees, reporting commitments |
| Record of holding | Share register statement or certificate | Registered charge, or a trust declaration naming your interest | Name, class, number of shares or dollar interest |
| Periodic statements | Distribution statement each distribution period | Payment statements from the mortgage administrator | Amount paid, amount reinvested, any arrears |
| Annual financial statements | Audited statements of the whole MIC | Not usually provided; you see the single loan instead | Auditor's opinion, impaired loans, allowance for credit losses |
| Tax slip | T5 for distributions on non-registered holdings | Depends on how the interest is held | Amount reported and how it is characterised |
| Loan file | A portfolio summary rather than individual loan files | Commitment, appraisal, title search, insurance | Loan-to-value, position, maturity date |

## How do I monitor my mortgage investment?

You monitor a mortgage investment by reading each document against the last one and against what the OM said would happen: is income arriving as described, is the portfolio still what you bought into, and has anything changed about getting your money back? Four habits cover most of it:

1. **Reconcile distributions.** Check each statement against your holding and the declared rate; a rate change, skipped distribution or unrequested switch between cash and reinvestment is worth a question.
2. **Read the notes, not just the totals.** The notes to the audited financial statements disclose impaired loans, the allowance for credit losses, the split between first and second mortgages, concentration by region and the maturity profile. The guide to [reading a MIC's financial statements and offering memorandum](/learn/reading-mic-financial-statements/) walks through each note.
3. **Watch the audit itself.** A qualified opinion, a change of auditor or late financial statements are signals in their own right.
4. **Track redemption terms.** A notice that redemptions are deferred, prorated or suspended changes the liquidity of what you hold; see [liquidity and redemption: getting your money out](/learn/liquidity-and-redemption/).

Mortgage investments are not guaranteed. Returns are targets, not promises, and principal can be lost. Monitoring does not change that risk; it shortens the time between something going wrong and you knowing about it.

### Worked example (illustrative)

Assume an investor holds $50,000 of MIC shares and, for this example only, the MIC declares a constant 8% annual distribution, paid quarterly and already net of its management fees and expenses. The investor uses the dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP). These are assumptions for checking arithmetic, not a forecast, and a higher target return generally comes with higher risk.

- Quarterly rate: 8% ÷ 4 = 2%.
- Quarter 1: $50,000.00 × 2% = $1,000.00, reinvested. The holding becomes $51,000.00.
- Quarter 2: $51,000.00 × 2% = $1,020.00. The holding becomes $52,020.00.
- Quarter 3: $52,020.00 × 2% = $1,040.40. The holding becomes $53,060.40.
- Quarter 4: $53,060.40 × 2% = $1,061.21 (rounded). The holding becomes $54,121.61.
- Distributions for the year: $1,000.00 + $1,020.00 + $1,040.40 + $1,061.21 = $4,121.61.

In a non-registered account, the year-end T5 would report $4,121.61 even though no cash was received, because reinvested distributions are still taxable. At an assumed 40% marginal tax rate, the tax is $4,121.61 × 40% = $1,648.64, payable from other money. Checking each statement against this kind of calculation is the simplest form of monitoring.

## How is MIC income reported for tax?

As at October 2026, a taxable dividend from a MIC, other than a capital gains dividend, is deemed under subsection 130.1(2) of the *Income Tax Act* to be received as interest on a bond issued by the MIC. In practice it is reported on a T5 and taxed as interest at your marginal rate, with no dividend gross-up or dividend tax credit, even though the issuer calls the payment a dividend. The details are in [how mortgage investment income is taxed](/learn/mortgage-investment-tax-treatment/); confirm your own treatment with a Canadian tax professional.

In a registered plan the distribution is paid into the plan rather than reported on your personal return that year, and the trustee's statement becomes the main record.

## Who handles the money behind the statements?

Behind every statement is a mortgage administrator collecting payments from borrowers and holding them in trust before they reach the lender or investors. In Ontario, mortgage administrators are licensed by the Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario (FSRA) under the *Mortgage Brokerages, Lenders and Administrators Act, 2006*, whose regulations set their record-keeping and reporting duties; FSRA publishes the current requirements. In British Columbia, the *Mortgage Services Act* is scheduled to come into force on 13 October 2026 and makes mortgage administration a licensed activity; check BCFSA for the licensing categories. Other provinces differ. This regulatory summary is current as of October 2026, and the role is explained further in [mortgage administrators: who services your investment](/learn/mortgage-administrators/).

As one example, Lendmax Capital MIC's mortgages are administered by Lendmax Inc. under FSRA Mortgage Administrator Licence 13002, with payments collected into trust and reconciled monthly; a licence number can be checked on the regulator's public register.

## What communication should I expect when something changes?

Expect written notice of anything that changes your rights or income: a reduced or suspended distribution, deferred redemptions, an amended OM, or a change of auditor or management. Many MICs, Lendmax Capital MIC among them, give the board power to defer or suspend redemptions, and a notice should say which power is being used and why.

Mortgage investments are illiquid: there is generally no secondary market for MIC shares, redemptions are subject to notice periods, and they can be delayed, gated or suspended, leaving money unavailable for longer than planned. The guide to [how to invest in mortgages step by step](/how-to-invest/) shows where these terms sit in the documents you sign.

### Common reporting mistakes

- **Filing statements unread.** Problems usually show up first as small changes: a lower distribution, late financial statements, a new impaired-loan note.
- **Reading a distribution statement as a valuation.** It shows what was paid, not what the loans would fetch.
- **Claiming the dividend tax credit on MIC income.** See the tax section above.
- **Treating a redemption request as a withdrawal.** It is a request, processed under the OM's terms.

## Mortgage investment reporting to investors: questions to ask before you subscribe

Each answer should appear in a named document:

- How often are statements issued, and when are audited financial statements delivered? *Offering memorandum.*
- Which portfolio statistics are disclosed: loan-to-value spread, position, arrears, regional concentration, maturities? *Notes to the audited financial statements.*
- How are distribution changes and redemption deferrals communicated? *Offering memorandum, redemption section.*
- Who administers the mortgages, under what licence, and is the dealer registered? *Offering memorandum; the provincial register; the CSA National Registration Search.*

A missing answer is information too. Terms used here are defined in the [mortgage investment glossary](/glossary/).

## What this means for a mortgage investor

Reporting is how an investor sees a mortgage investment after the money has left their account, so its quality is worth judging before subscribing, not after. The documents differ by structure, but the habit is constant: compare each statement with the last one and with the OM's commitments, and treat delays, rate changes and redemption notices as information. What the reports describe varies along seven axes — the borrower, the property, the loan-to-value, the security position, the term, the jurisdiction and the investment structure — and useful reporting lets an investor see each of them.
