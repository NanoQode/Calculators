General liability insurance cost in Canada depends mostly on what your business does, how big it is and the limit you choose. For a $2 million limit, Instasure's estimate tool puts a small consulting firm at about $386 a year and a small restaurant at about $1,924. Below you'll find a dated table by limit and business type, the factors that move the price, and ways to pay less without cutting the cover you need.

## How much does general liability insurance cost in Canada?

For a $2 million limit, Instasure's estimate tool puts general liability insurance cost at $386 a year for an example consulting firm and $1,924 a year for an example restaurant. Both are CGL-only estimates for Ontario profiles with $150,000 of revenue and one employee, as of October 2026.

That's about $32 a month for the consultant and $160 a month for the restaurant. A skilled trade with the same revenue and staff comes in at $1,562 a year.

Commercial general liability (CGL) is the core liability policy for most businesses. The [Insurance Bureau of Canada (IBC) glossary](https://www.ibc.ca/insurance-basics/glossary) describes it as broad coverage for bodily injury or property damage to others that arises from your business operations. IBC's guide to [buying business insurance](https://www.ibc.ca/insurance-basics/business/how-to-buy-business-insurance) adds that liability coverage typically pays the legal costs of defending a lawsuit, as well as damages you're found liable for.

### Cost by limit: $1 million vs $2 million vs $5 million

Your limit is the most an insurer will pay. In the model, dropping from $2 million to $1 million lowers the estimate by about 15%. Moving up to $5 million adds about 35%. So the $2 million liability insurance cost for the example consultant is $58 a year more than the $1 million cost.

Many businesses choose $2 million because contracts ask for it. The federal government's [standard clause for commercial general liability](https://buyandsell.gc.ca/policy-and-guidelines/standard-acquisition-clauses-and-conditions-manual/5/g/g2001c/1) asks contractors for a limit usual for that kind of contract. The floor is $2,000,000 per accident or occurrence and in the annual aggregate. Contracting officers are told to get risk management advice if $2 million doesn't look adequate.

Construction work can ask for more. One [RCMP construction tender posted on CanadaBuys](https://canadabuys.canada.ca/sites/default/files/webform/tender_notice/6040/sample_m5000-23-5046b_itt-upgrade-exterior-drainage-system.pdf) set a $5,000,000 each-occurrence limit. If a contract asks for a high limit, ask whether an [umbrella insurance](/umbrella-insurance/) or excess policy on top of your CGL can meet it.

### Cost by business type and revenue

The type of work moves the price more than anything else. At a $2 million limit and the smaller size, a restaurant's estimate is about five times a consultant's. A skilled trade's is about four times.

That's the short answer to how much is liability insurance for a small business: it depends on the risk your work creates for other people. A consultant rarely has customers on site. A restaurant has a kitchen, a dining room and customers every day. A contractor works in other people's homes and buildings, and finished work can fail after the job ends. If you're in the trades, see our page on [contractor insurance](/contractor-insurance/). Food businesses can start with [restaurant insurance](/business-insurance/restaurants/).

Size matters too. In the model, a business with $1,000,000 of revenue and five employees gets a little more than double the estimate of a $150,000, one-employee business of the same type.

For property, cyber and full package pricing by industry, see our guide to [small business insurance cost](/guides/small-business-insurance-cost-canada/).

## Deductibles, aggregates and add-ons that change the price

### Per-occurrence and aggregate limits

A CGL policy usually has two limits. The per-occurrence limit is the most it pays for one incident. The aggregate limit is the most it pays for all claims in the policy year. The federal clause above sets the same $2,000,000 for both. Read your contract's wording, because some ask for a higher aggregate.

### Endorsements your contracts may require

Contracts often ask for more than a limit. The federal CGL clause lists endorsements such as:

- **Additional insured:** the client is added to your policy, but only for liability arising from your own negligence on the contract.
- **Cross liability:** each insured is treated as if it had its own policy.
- **Blanket contractual liability:** cover for liability you take on under a contract.
- **Notice of cancellation:** the insurer agrees to give the client 30 days' written notice before cancelling.

Ask whether each one is included or costs extra. Send the insurance clause to your licensed advisor before you get quotes.

### Packages and add-ons

CGL alone doesn't cover everything. IBC notes that risks such as advice you give, business vehicles and lost income after a loss need separate cover. That means [professional liability insurance](/professional-liability-insurance/), [commercial auto insurance](/business-insurance/commercial-auto/) and business interruption. Businesses that hold customer data may also look at [cyber insurance](/business-insurance/cyber/).

Here's how the estimate tool prices each step for one example: an Ontario consultant with $150,000 of revenue, one employee and a $2 million limit.

## How to lower CGL cost without cutting cover

These steps cut cost without leaving a gap you can't afford.

1. **Match the limit to your contracts.** Check what your clients, lease and tenders require before you buy more or less.
2. **Describe your work precisely.** Insurers rate the business you tell them about, so a wrong description can put a claim at risk.
3. **Ask for two deductibles.** Compare the saving with what you could pay after a claim.
4. **Price a package and stand-alone policies.** If you need property and business interruption anyway, compare both ways.
5. **Collect certificates from subcontractors.** Make sure the people you hire carry their own liability cover.
6. **Update revenue and staff at renewal.** Pricing follows size, so keep the numbers current, and compare insurers while you're at it.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I treat CGL as a cost of doing business, on the same line as rent or software. The table shows it's a modest line for low-risk work and a real one for trades and restaurants. My rule of thumb is to start with the limit your contracts and lease require. Then ask a harder question: could one bad claim cost more than that? If it could, price the next limit up before you chase a smaller premium.

I'd also look at liability in the context of the whole household plan. For many self-employed people, the business budget and the family budget are the same budget. A claim your policy doesn't cover can come out of savings meant for the mortgage, the kids or retirement. That's why I'd rather see an accurate description of your work and a sensible limit than a policy bought on price alone. Buy what protects the plan, not what is sold hardest.
