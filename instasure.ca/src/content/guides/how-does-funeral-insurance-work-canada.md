How does funeral insurance work? You buy a small permanent life insurance policy, often $5,000 to $25,000, and pay the same premium every month. When you die, the insurer pays that amount to the person you named, who uses it for the funeral and final bills. Below you'll find what it pays for, the two main types, dated example prices by age and how a claim is paid.

## How does funeral insurance work?

Funeral insurance is a small whole life policy sized to your funeral. You answer a few health questions, or none, and pay a level premium. When you die, your beneficiary sends the insurer a claim form and proof of death. The insurer pays a tax-free lump sum, and your beneficiary pays the funeral home.

Here is the whole process in five steps:

| Step | What happens | Who does it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Apply | Choose an amount, answer the health questions (or none, for guaranteed issue) and name a beneficiary | You |
| 2. Pay premiums | The premium stays the same; in the two guaranteed-issue plans we checked, payments stop at age 95 and the cover continues | You |
| 3. Claim | Send the insurer its claim form and proof of death, such as a death certificate | Your beneficiary |
| 4. Payout | The insurer pays the benefit as a lump sum, directly to the named beneficiary | The insurer |
| 5. Funeral | Pay the funeral home, cemetery or crematorium, plus any final bills | Your beneficiary |

You'll also see it sold as burial insurance in Canada, or as [funeral expense insurance](/life-insurance/funeral-expense/). The names differ, but the product is the same: a small, lifelong policy whose payout is meant for the funeral.

## What funeral expense insurance pays for

The policy pays cash, not services. That gives your family room to choose. They can put the money toward:

- funeral home services, a casket or an urn
- a burial plot, a cremation, a headstone or a memorial
- a reception and travel for family
- the last utility, phone and credit card bills

The [Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC)](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/insurance/life.html) describes a life insurance death benefit as a one-time, tax-free payment. Paid to a named beneficiary, it doesn't wait for the estate to be settled. If you don't name anyone, the insurer pays your estate, and the FCAC notes the money may then be subject to estate taxes. So name a person, and update the name if your life changes.

How much cover do you need? Funeral prices vary widely by province, funeral home and choices such as burial or cremation. We couldn't confirm a national average from a primary source, so we won't quote one. Instead, ask two or three funeral homes for a written price list. Add your final bills, then subtract any savings set aside for the purpose.

### How the CPP death benefit fits in

Government help is a fixed, modest amount. The [CPP death benefit](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/cpp-death-benefit.html) is a one-time payment of $2,500 when the person contributed enough to the Canada Pension Plan. For deaths on or after January 1, 2025, there is a possible $2,500 top-up, for a maximum of $5,000.

The top-up has two conditions. The person must never have received a CPP or QPP retirement pension, disability benefit or post-retirement disability benefit. And they must not leave a spouse or common-law partner who is eligible for a survivor's pension. So if the person was already receiving a CPP retirement pension, the estate gets the basic $2,500 only.

To qualify at all, the person must have contributed for at least one-third of the calendar years in their contributory period (and no fewer than three), or for 10 calendar years. The benefit goes to the estate, and Service Canada says the executor should apply within 60 days of the death. Insurance, by contrast, goes straight to your beneficiary. Our guide to the [CPP death benefit and funeral costs](/guides/cpp-death-benefit-and-funeral-costs/) walks through the application and how to plan around the gap.

## Simplified issue vs guaranteed issue

Funeral policies come in two main types. The difference is how the insurer decides to accept you, and what happens if you die early in the policy.

| Feature | Simplified issue | Guaranteed issue |
|---|---|---|
| Health questions | A short list, no medical exam | None |
| Who is accepted | People who can answer the questions favourably | Anyone within the plan's age limits |
| Death from illness in the first two years | Usually the full benefit, once approved | Usually a refund of premiums instead of the benefit |
| Accidental death in the first two years | Full benefit | Usually covered |
| Price for the same profile | Lower | Higher: about 45% more in Instasure's model |

Two published plans show how the guaranteed-issue limit works. [RBC Insurance](https://www.rbcinsurance.com/en-ca/life-insurance/guaranteed-acceptance-disclosure/) says its Guaranteed Acceptance Life plan accepts applicants aged 40 to 75, for $5,000 to $40,000. If the insured dies within two years, the benefit is limited to a refund of premiums without interest, unless the death is accidental. [Sun Life](https://www.sunlife.ca/en/insurance/life/permanent-life-insurance/sun-life-go-guaranteed/) says its Go Guaranteed plan accepts ages 30 to 74, for $5,000 to $25,000. It won't pay a claim in the first two years unless the death is accidental, and refunds premiums less any administrative fees.

Some insurers also sort applicants into in-between plans based on their health answers. These can pay a reduced benefit in the first year or two. Read the benefit schedule before you sign.

Whatever the type, answer every question truthfully. The FCAC's [guide to making an insurance claim](https://www.canada.ca/en/financial-consumer-agency/services/insurance/make-claim.html) notes a life insurer can deny a claim within the contestability period. A misstatement on the application can lead to a denied claim. If you can't answer health questions at all, compare [no-medical life insurance](/no-medical-life-insurance/) options with a licensed advisor before you settle.

*Instasure estimates for an example profile (non-smoker, $10,000 funeral expense policy, level premiums), as of October 2026. Ranges show the spread in our model. Guaranteed-issue plans often stop accepting new applicants in the mid-70s; the two plans above do. The last column divides $10,000 by a year of the simplified-issue premium.*

For a 50-year-old woman, $29.50 a month works out to about $0.97 a day. That's the example behind the "from $1 a day" figure on our funeral expense page.

What moves the price, in our model:

- **Age:** the price rises about 50% from 50 to 60, then 70% to 90% in each decade after that. Buying earlier locks in a lower level premium.
- **Smoking:** the 50-year-old woman pays $39.50 a month as a smoker, instead of $29.50.
- **Amount:** $20,000 for a 60-year-old woman is $86 a month, a little under twice the $10,000 price.
- **Health answers:** guaranteed issue costs more because the insurer accepts everyone.

### Funeral insurance for seniors

The last column matters most after 70. A 70-year-old man would pay premiums equal to the $10,000 benefit in about nine years. At 80, it takes about five. That doesn't make the policy a bad idea. It means you're paying for certainty, so compare it with simply setting money aside. For wider options after 60, see [life insurance for seniors](/life-insurance/seniors/).

## How is a funeral insurance claim paid, and how fast?

A claim is usually simple, because the amount is small and fixed. Here's what your beneficiary does:

1. Find the policy number and call the insurer, or the licensed advisor who sold the policy.
2. Complete the insurer's claim form.
3. Send proof of death, such as a copy of the death certificate.
4. Wait for the insurer's review. The FCAC says the insurer may look into the circumstances of a claim, and there may be a delay before payment.
5. Receive the benefit as a lump sum, then pay the funeral home and final bills.

Speed depends mostly on timing and paperwork. A claim on a policy that has been in force for years, with a named beneficiary, is usually the quickest. A death early in the policy can take longer. The insurer may review the application during the contestability period, and on guaranteed-issue plans the cause of death decides whether the full benefit is paid.

Tell your beneficiary where the policy is kept. If a claim is denied, the FCAC says to start with the insurer's complaint process. You can then go to the OmbudService for Life and Health Insurance or your provincial regulator.

## When funeral insurance is the wrong tool

Funeral insurance does one job well. It's the wrong tool in these cases:

- **You still have a mortgage or dependants.** A small policy won't cover them. [Term life insurance](/term-life-insurance/) buys far more cover per dollar. Use the [life insurance calculator](/calculators/life-insurance-needs/) to see how much life insurance you need.
- **You want more than the funeral covered.** [Final expense insurance](/life-insurance/final-expense/) leaves room for debts and estate costs. Larger needs may call for [whole life insurance](/whole-life-insurance/); our [term vs whole life insurance](/guides/term-vs-whole-life-insurance-canada/) guide explains the trade-off.
- **You're healthy and in your 50s.** In our model, a fully underwritten small whole life policy runs about 15% less than simplified issue. See [how much life insurance costs](/guides/how-much-does-life-insurance-cost-canada/) for more examples.
- **You want specific services at today's prices.** A pre-arranged funeral contract with a funeral home buys set services under your province's rules. Insurance pays cash your family can use with any provider.
- **You already have savings set aside.** If money for the funeral is already there, a policy adds cost without adding much.

## Quebec and the other provinces

Funeral insurance works the same way across Canada, and Instasure's advisor service covers every province except Quebec and the territories. In Quebec, this guide is information only. Service Canada says to contact Retraite Québec about the death benefit if the person lived in Quebec when they died, or contributed only to the Quebec Pension Plan.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I'd start with the funeral itself, not the policy. Get a written price list, write down your wishes, and subtract what's already there: savings, the basic CPP death benefit if you qualify, and any existing life insurance. Only the gap needs insuring. A policy sized to that gap is a small, honest piece of the plan.

My rule of thumb: if you can answer health questions, don't settle for guaranteed issue. You'd pay more and usually wait two years for full cover. And if you're in your 70s or 80s, run the numbers in the last column before you buy. Sometimes a dedicated savings account does the same job.
