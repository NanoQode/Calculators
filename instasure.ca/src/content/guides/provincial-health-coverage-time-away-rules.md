How long can you leave Canada and keep health insurance from your province? It depends on where you live. Most provincial and territorial plans let you be away for about six to seven months a year, and several allow a longer absence if you arrange it first. But every plan pays only a small part of emergency bills outside Canada, so the time limit is only half of the question.

Below is one table with the rules for all 13 plans, then extended absences, payments abroad, US day counts and a snowbird checklist.

## How long can you leave Canada and keep health insurance?

Most plans let you be away for about six to seven months in a year, as long as your home stays in the province. Quebec allows fewer than 183 days outside the province per calendar year. Ontario allows up to 212 days in any 12-month period. BC, Saskatchewan and Nova Scotia allow seven months of travel, and Manitoba seven months outside Canada.

Two details trip people up. First, the clock usually runs on days outside your province, not just days outside Canada, so a summer trip within Canada can use up part of your allowance. Second, many plans want notice before a long trip, even when you stay within the limit.

### Calendar year or any 12 months?

There's no single snowbird six-month rule in Canada, and how your plan counts days matters as much as the limit itself. Quebec, BC and Nova Scotia count within a calendar year. Ontario and Alberta look at any 12-month period, so a long winter that crosses January doesn't reset on New Year's Day.

Quebec has two counting rules worth knowing. Absences of 21 consecutive days or less don't count, and neither do your departure and return dates. A short spring trip can therefore sit outside your Quebec tally, while the same trip could count in Ontario.

## Extended absences: one-time and repeat options

Several plans offer an extended absence: a longer period away during which you keep your card. Each has conditions, and most must be arranged before you leave.

- **Ontario:** outside Canada more than seven months in a 12-month period? You may keep OHIP for up to two years if you meet the residence conditions in the table.
- **British Columbia:** up to 24 consecutive months, once in a 60-month period. You must have been physically present in Canada for six of the 12 months before departure.
- **Quebec:** once every seven years, you may spend 183 days or more outside Quebec in a calendar year and stay eligible.
- **Nova Scotia:** a 12-month absence once every six years.
- **New Brunswick:** up to 12 months of coverage once every three years, counted from your return.

An extended absence protects your eligibility. It doesn't change what your plan pays for care abroad, which is where the real cost sits.

## What each plan pays for care outside Canada

Plans pay for emergency care abroad at home rates, not foreign prices. That's the core of out of country health coverage by province. Here are the published daily amounts we found, as of October 2026:

| Plan | Emergency inpatient care, per day | Outpatient care |
|---|---|---|
| Ontario (OHIP) | $400 in intensive, coronary, operating-room or special care units. $200 for lower levels of care. | $50 a day or the hospital bill, whichever is less. |
| British Columbia (MSP) | Up to $75. | Not stated on the page we checked. |
| Quebec (RAMQ) | Up to $100. | Up to $50 a day at a hospital outpatient clinic. |
| Newfoundland and Labrador (MCP) | Up to $350 in a community or regional hospital. Up to $465 in a tertiary or specialized hospital. | Up to $62 a day. |

*Canadian dollars, from each plan's official pages as of October 2026. The other plans give no daily figure on the pages we checked.*

RAMQ gives a plain example of the gap. For a three-day intensive care stay in Florida with a hospital bill of $25,000, it reimbursed $300. The rest fell to the traveller or their private insurer.

Other limits matter too. Nova Scotia doesn't pay outpatient charges abroad, and Quebec, Yukon and Nunavut don't cover ambulance charges. Planned care abroad needs approval before you go in Ontario and BC. Most plans' own pages advise buying private travel insurance before you leave.

## How time away interacts with US stay limits

Your province counts days outside the province. The United States keeps its own counts for entry and for tax.

For tax, the IRS uses the substantial presence test. On its Form 8840, you meet the test if you were in the US for at least 31 days in the current year and 183 days over the current year and the two before it. Days in the current year count in full, a third of last year's days count, and a sixth of the year before. Someone who meets the test can still claim the closer connection exception on Form 8840, filed separately for each year, if they spent fewer than 183 days in the US that year and meet the other conditions.

This is not tax or immigration advice. How long you may stay as a visitor is a question for US Customs and Border Protection, and tax status is one for the IRS, the Canada Revenue Agency or a cross-border tax professional. Staying within your provincial limit doesn't settle your US position, and the reverse is also true.

## Snowbird planning checklist

Use this checklist for snowbird provincial health rules a few months before you book.

1. **Find your plan's limit and counting period.** Check whether your plan counts the calendar year or any 12 months, and add up every trip outside your province, not only the winter one.
2. **Give notice on time.** Manitoba asks for 6 to 8 weeks' notice, New Brunswick wants to hear about any absence of 30 days or more, and Newfoundland and Labrador issues a certificate for trips over 30 days.
3. **Decide early on an extended absence.** If you might stay longer than the usual limit, apply before you leave, and note how often your plan allows it.
4. **Keep proof of residence.** Note each departure and return date. The NWT, for example, asks snowbirds for a recent tax form and a statutory declaration.
5. **Buy coverage for the whole stay.** A policy shorter than your trip leaves the last weeks uninsured. If you rely on a credit card or group plan, check its trip length and top it up if needed.
6. **Answer the medical questionnaire carefully.** A medication change or new symptom before you leave can affect coverage for that condition.
7. **Know the claim deadlines.** Alberta wants out-of-country claims within 365 days, and Nova Scotia doesn't accept claims after 6 months. Ask for itemized hospital bills: Newfoundland and Labrador requires a detailed statement.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I treat the time-away limit as a hard line in a retirement budget, not a target. My rule of thumb is to plan your longest trip to end a few weeks before your plan's limit. That way a delayed flight or a change in plans doesn't push you over. I'd also count days the way your plan counts them, because a calendar-year plan and a 12-month plan can give different answers for the same winter.

The bigger risk to the plan isn't the days, it's the bill. The daily amounts in this guide are small next to a foreign hospital stay, so I'd budget private travel coverage as a fixed part of the winter, like rent or flights. Buy it for the full stay before you book non-refundable travel. And keep tax and border questions with the professionals who handle them, separate from the health plan question.
