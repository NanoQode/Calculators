How much is tenant insurance in Canada? For one example renter, Instasure's model puts it at about $20 to $26 a month across the provinces, as of October 2026. That's less than a dollar a day for contents, liability and living expenses after a fire. Below you'll find a dated table by province and city, what moves the price, and how to set your limits.

## How much is tenant insurance in Canada?

For an example renter with $30,000 of contents, $2 million liability and a $1,000 deductible, Instasure's model estimates tenant insurance at $20 to $26 a month across the 10 provinces. That's about $240 to $312 a year, as of October 2026. Ontario has the highest provincial estimate, and the territories run $28 to $30 a month.

There's no official national figure to check this against. Statistics Canada publishes price indexes for homeowners' and car insurance. But its 2025 paper on [insurance price indexes](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/62f0014m/62f0014m2025002-eng.htm) explains that the tenants' insurance price isn't measured directly. It is imputed from household furnishings and equipment prices, because price data of sufficient quality aren't available.

Published figures for renters insurance cost in Canada rest on different profiles too. This guide gives you one stated profile, dated, in every province and major city.

### Average tenant insurance in Ontario

The example Ontario estimate is $26 a month, with a range of $20 to $33, as of October 2026. Ottawa matches it, and Toronto is $27 because of the flood loading. Other published Ontario figures use their own renter profiles, so compare like with like: the same contents, liability limit and deductible.

Ontario's [standard lease](https://www.ontario.ca/page/guide-ontarios-standard-lease) has a tenant insurance section. The landlord and tenant can agree that the tenant must carry liability insurance, and the landlord can ask for proof. Contents insurance is the tenant's choice. ## What changes the price

These are the main inputs, drawn from IBC's guidance and our model:

- **Contents value.** The more your belongings would cost to replace, the higher the premium.
- **Location.** The number, type and cost of claims in your neighbourhood.
- **Claims history.** Past claims are often an insurer's strongest predictor of future claims.
- **Coverage type.** [IBC](https://www.ibc.ca/insurance-basics/home/types-of-home-insurance-coverage) notes renters can usually choose all-risks or named-perils contents cover, and all-risks can raise the premium.
- **Deductible and add-ons.** A higher deductible lowers the price; water endorsements raise it.

## Choosing contents and liability limits

The right tenant insurance cost starts with the right limits. Set these three before you compare prices:

1. **Contents.** Walk through each room and add up what it would cost to buy everything again. Our [tenant and condo coverage calculator](/calculators/tenant-condo-coverage/) does the adding for you. Count clothing, kitchenware and electronics too, because they add up.
2. **Liability.** IBC says a standard tenant policy generally covers up to $1 million if you're sued for injuring someone or damaging their property. You can often raise it to $2 million. Liability coverage also helps pay for your legal defence. If your lease names a minimum, carry at least that much.
3. **Additional living expenses.** If an insured loss makes your unit unlivable, this pays necessary extra costs while it's repaired, such as a hotel. Check the limit in your policy.

Two more checks. IBC lists sewer backup as an optional add-on, so ask for it if you live in a basement or ground-floor unit. Our guide to [does home insurance cover water damage](/guides/does-home-insurance-cover-water-damage-canada/) explains the water endorsements. And your policy covers only the items you own, so IBC suggests each roommate get their own policy.

## Ways to pay less on tenant insurance per month

Five levers move your premium without leaving gaps:

1. **Choose a deductible you can cover.** IBC says a higher deductible lowers the premium. Pick one your emergency fund could pay the week after a loss.
2. **Bundle with your car.** IBC suggests bundling car and home policies and asking about a multi-policy discount. Get [car insurance quotes](/quote/car-insurance/) at the same time so you can compare a bundle with separate policies.
3. **Insure the right contents amount.** In the Ontario example, each extra $10,000 of contents adds about $3 to $5 a month. Insure what you own, not a round number.
4. **Save claims for real losses.** Past claims raise the price in our model, and IBC says insurers treat them as a key predictor. Paying for small losses yourself can protect your premium.
5. **Compare at renewal.** Each insurer weighs these factors its own way. Get fresh quotes before your policy renews.

Don't cut liability or water cover to save a dollar. Dropping to named perils saves about $5 a month in our Ontario example, but it narrows what's covered.

## How we built these estimates

- **Source.** All Instasure figures come from our estimate engine, last reviewed in October 2026. They are modelled estimates for an example profile, not quotes, offers or averages.
- **Method.** The model starts from a provincial benchmark for $30,000 of contents and $2 million liability. It scales with the square root of your contents value, so doubling contents raises the price by about 40%. It adds about 20% for each past claim and a small hail or flood loading in some cities.
- **Range.** The range runs from about 22% below to 26% above the typical figure, to reflect differences between insurers and buildings.
- **Outside data.** We don't show other sites' tenant averages here, because each rests on its own undisclosed profile.
- **Updates.** We plan to refresh this guide every quarter. The [Instasure Rate Index](/insights/rate-index/) will add median figures from real quote requests once a province has at least 25 requests.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I see tenant insurance as a small line in a renter's budget that protects two big things: everything you own, and your savings if you're sued. For the example renter in our model, it costs less than a dollar a day. My rule of thumb is to buy it the week you sign the lease, before you move a single box.

Where I'd spend your attention is the limits, not the last dollar of premium. Do the room-by-room count once, keep a photo inventory on your phone, and choose a deductible your emergency fund can absorb. Then review it whenever you move or make a big purchase, such as a new laptop or bike.
