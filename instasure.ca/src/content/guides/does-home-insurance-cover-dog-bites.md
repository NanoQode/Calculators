Does home insurance cover dog bites? Usually, yes. When your dog injures someone else, the personal liability section of your home, condo or tenant policy is what responds, up to your limit. Ontario makes dog owners liable without proof of fault, while other provinces use different rules.

## Does home insurance cover dog bites?

Usually, yes. If your dog bites a visitor, a neighbour or someone at the park, the personal liability coverage in your home, condo or tenant policy responds. It pays what you're legally liable for, plus your legal defence, up to your limit, unless your wording excludes animals. Answer your insurer's questions about pets accurately.

## How personal liability responds to a dog bite claim

The [Insurance Bureau of Canada (IBC)](https://www.ibc.ca/insurance-basics/home/types-of-home-insurance-coverage) describes personal liability as coverage for bodily injury or property damage you unintentionally cause to others. It applies on your property or anywhere in the world. Up to the limit you choose, it pays what you'd owe someone who successfully sues you. It also pays the cost of defending you.

If you're shopping for dog liability insurance, start with the liability section of the [home insurance](/home-insurance/) policy you already have. Three limits matter most:

- **Your household isn't covered.** Liability coverage doesn't apply to injuries to you or members of your household.
- **Your limit is the ceiling.** A serious injury can lead to a claim larger than your limit. IBC notes that you can often increase it.
- **Wording varies.** IBC notes that what individual policies cover varies from one insurer to another. Read the exclusions for animals before you need them.

### Looking after someone else's dog

Ontario's Dog Owners' Liability Act defines an owner to include a person who possesses or harbours the dog. So in Ontario, dog-sitting can make you liable for a bite too. Ask your insurer whether your liability coverage applies to a dog you care for but don't own.

### Pet insurance is for your own vet bills

[Pet insurance](/pet-insurance/) reimburses part of your own pet's vet bills. Unless a plan's wording expressly includes third-party liability, don't count on it when your dog injures someone.

## Ontario's Dog Owners' Liability Act

Ontario has a law written just for dogs, the [Dog Owners' Liability Act](https://www.ontario.ca/laws/statute/90d16). These are its main rules:

- **Owners are liable for bites and attacks.** The owner of a dog is liable for damages resulting from a bite or attack on another person or domestic animal.
- **No proof of fault is needed.** Liability doesn't depend on the owner knowing the dog's propensity, or on fault or negligence. In plain terms, that's strict liability.
- **Shared fault lowers the award.** A court must reduce damages to the degree the injured person's own fault or negligence caused or contributed to them.
- **Co-owners share liability.** Where a dog has more than one owner, they're jointly and severally liable.
- **It applies at home too.** On the owner's premises, this Act decides liability rather than the Occupiers' Liability Act. There's a narrow exception for a person on the premises to commit a criminal act.
- **Owners must take precautions.** An owner must exercise reasonable precautions to prevent the dog from biting, attacking or menacing. A court can order control measures, or order the dog destroyed.
- **Pit bulls are banned.** Except as the Act or its regulations permit, no one may own or breed a pit bull. Ontario's [animal welfare page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/animal-welfare) still lists pit bulls as banned under the Act.

### What it means for home insurance in Ontario

Because liability doesn't depend on fault, a bite claim can reach your policy even if your dog has never shown aggression. Check your liability limit first. Compare options for [home insurance in Ontario](/home-insurance/ontario/), or see local figures for [home insurance in Toronto](/home-insurance/ontario/toronto/).

## Other provinces: fault-based rules, with two exceptions

Two other provinces have their own rules on animal owners' liability:

- **Manitoba.** The [Animal Liability Act](https://web2.gov.mb.ca/laws/statutes/ccsm/_pdf.php?cap=A95) covers animals generally, not just dogs. An owner is liable for damage an animal does to people or property, whether or not the owner knew its tendencies or was negligent. Damages are reduced for the injured person's own fault.
- **Quebec (information only).** Article 1466 of the [Civil Code of Québec](https://www.canlii.org/en/qc/laws/stat/cqlr-c-ccq-1991/latest/cqlr-c-ccq-1991.html) makes the owner of an animal liable for injury it causes. That applies whether the animal was in the owner's custody, someone else's, or had strayed or escaped. A person using the animal can also be liable during that time.

Elsewhere, unless a provincial statute says otherwise, claims generally turn on fault-based common-law rules. The injured person generally has to prove negligence. Or they rely on the old scienter rule: liability where the owner knew the animal had a dangerous tendency. The Supreme Court of Canada described that rule in [Fleming v. Atkinson (1959)](https://www.canlii.org/en/ca/scc/doc/1959/1959canlii10/1959canlii10.html). On your own property, occupiers' liability law can also apply.

Even a claim you defeat has to be defended, and your liability coverage pays defence costs for covered claims. If you live in British Columbia, compare liability limits on [home insurance in BC](/home-insurance/british-columbia/).

## Dog bite liability by province: the rules at a glance

| Province | Main rule for a dog bite | Must the injured person prove fault? | What your liability section pays |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ontario | Dog Owners' Liability Act (dogs only). | No. The award is reduced for their own fault. | Damages and defence costs, up to your limit. |
| Manitoba | Animal Liability Act (all animals). | No. The award is reduced for their own fault. | Damages and defence costs, up to your limit. |
| Quebec (information only) | Civil Code of Québec, article 1466. | Not under the article's wording: the owner must repair injury the animal causes, even if it strayed or escaped. | Damages and defence costs, up to your limit. |
| BC, Alberta, Saskatchewan, New Brunswick, Nova Scotia, PEI, Newfoundland and Labrador | Common law, unless a provincial statute says otherwise. | Generally yes: negligence, or your knowledge of the dog's dangerous tendency (scienter). | Damages and defence costs, up to your limit. |

*Summary of the cited statutes and case law, October 2026; general information, not legal advice. Other provinces and the territories have dog-control statutes and bylaws; we didn't confirm whether any of them changes the civil liability rule.*

## Breed restrictions, home insurance questions and exclusions

Insurers write their own policy wording and decide which risks they accept. So there's no single answer on breed restrictions in home insurance. Before you buy or renew, ask:

1. Does the application ask about dogs in the home, their breed or any bite history?
2. Does the policy exclude or limit liability for animals, or for particular breeds?
3. Does liability coverage apply to a dog you're caring for but don't own?

Answer every question accurately and keep the answers in writing. An inaccurate answer on an application can put a claim at risk.

## When umbrella liability helps

An [umbrella liability](/umbrella-insurance/) policy sits above the liability limits on your home and auto policies and pays once a covered claim exceeds them. Consider one if a serious injury claim could reach your savings, home equity or future income. Ask which underlying limits the umbrella insurer requires.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I see liability coverage as the part of insurance that protects everything else. Your savings, your home equity and your future income are what a serious injury claim would reach. If you live in Ontario or Manitoba, the law doesn't ask whether your dog has ever bitten before. My rule of thumb is to set your liability limit with your net worth and income in mind, not the default on the renewal notice.

I'd also do the paperwork while things are calm. Read the animal exclusions, answer the pet questions accurately, and confirm whether a dog you're minding is covered. When the gap between your limit and your exposure is wide, an umbrella policy can be a sensible add-on.
