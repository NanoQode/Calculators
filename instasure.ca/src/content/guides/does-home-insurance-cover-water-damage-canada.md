Does home insurance cover water damage? In Canada, a standard home policy usually covers water damage that is sudden and accidental, like a burst pipe or a split washing-machine hose. Damage that builds up slowly, water that backs up from the sewer, and flooding from outside are different: they are excluded or need optional coverage.

The rest of this guide sorts water damage by where the water came from. That is how insurers decide claims.

## The short answer depends on where the water came from

Insurers sort water claims by source. Here is how a typical Canadian home policy treats each one.

| Source of water | Standard home policy | How to get covered |
|---|---|---|
| Burst pipe or appliance leak (sudden) | Usually covered. | Included in most policies. |
| Slow leak, seepage, rot or mould from neglect | Excluded. | Not insurable. Fix it early. |
| Sewer or drain backup | Usually excluded. | Sewer backup endorsement. |
| Overland flood from rain, snowmelt or a river | Excluded. | Overland water endorsement, where offered. |
| Groundwater through the foundation | Usually excluded. | Limited cover from some insurers. |
| Rain or melting ice through the roof or windows | Often excluded. | Above-ground water endorsement, where offered. |
| Broken underground water or sewer line | Usually excluded. | Service line endorsement. |

Wording varies by insurer and province. Your policy wording and declarations page decide what is covered, not general rules. If you can't find them, ask your insurer or broker for a copy.

## Sudden and accidental vs gradual damage

Most home policies cover a sudden, accidental "escape of water" from plumbing, heating, air conditioning or appliances inside the home. Think of a supply line that bursts, a water heater that ruptures, or a dishwasher hose that splits. The policy usually pays to tear out and repair the damaged walls, floors and belongings.

Many policies won't pay to fix the failed pipe or appliance itself. They pay for the damage it caused.

Gradual damage is treated very differently. A drip under the sink for months, failed caulking around a tub, or slow seepage is normally excluded. Insurers see these as maintenance issues, not sudden events. Mould and rot that result from long-term moisture are usually excluded or tightly limited.

Freezing is a common trap. Many policies exclude frozen-pipe damage if the home was left without heat. Some also require someone to check the home regularly while you're away in the heating season. The exact rules differ, so read the freezing and vacancy conditions in your wording.

> **Tip:** Before a winter trip, read the freezing and vacancy conditions in your policy. Arrange for someone to check the home as often as the wording requires, and keep a simple log of their visits.

## What is sewer backup coverage?

Sewer backup coverage pays when water or sewage flows back into your home through floor drains, toilets or sump pits. This usually happens when municipal sewers are overwhelmed during a heavy storm.

In most of Canada, sewer backup is an optional endorsement. It often has its own limit, which can be lower than your main dwelling limit. It may also carry a higher deductible than the rest of your policy.

Insurers may ask about backwater valves and sump pumps before they offer this coverage. Some offer better terms when you have them. Some municipalities also offer subsidies for backwater valves, so check with your city before you install one.

## Overland flood coverage and the national program

Overland water coverage pays when surface water enters your home. Typical causes are heavy rainfall, rapid snowmelt, or a river or lake overflowing its banks. Standard policies exclude it, so you must add an endorsement.

Availability and price depend on your address. Insurers use flood maps and claims data. Homes in the highest-risk zones may be offered coverage at a very high price, a low limit, or not at all. Coastal storm surge may be treated separately, so coastal homeowners should ask about it directly.

Severe weather is a growing cost for insurers and homeowners. Severe weather caused more than $2.4 billion in insured losses in Canada in 2025, according to the [Insurance Bureau of Canada](https://www.ibc.ca/news-insights/news/severe-weather-related-insured-losses-in-canada-exceed-2-4-billion-in-2025). That figure covers all severe weather, not only floods.

The federal government had targeted April 2026 for a national flood insurance program for high-risk households. That target was missed. Mid-2026 reporting said no launch timeline had been set, leaving about 1.5 million high-risk households relying on private insurers ([Money.ca](https://money.ca/news/canada-national-flood-insurance-program-delay-homeowners)). We will update this guide when a launch date is confirmed.

## Groundwater, above-ground water and service lines

These three gaps are easy to miss.

### Groundwater

Groundwater is water that rises through the soil and seeps through foundation walls or floors. It is excluded by most standard wordings. A few insurers offer limited groundwater coverage as part of a water package. If your basement is finished, ask whether yours does.

### Above-ground water

Above-ground water is rain or melting snow that gets in through the roof, eavestroughs, windows or doors. Ice dams are a common cause. Many policies cover this only if the opening was first created by an insured peril, such as wind or hail tearing off shingles. Some insurers sell an above-ground water endorsement to close the gap.

### Service lines

Service lines are the underground pipes and cables that connect your home to the street. Repairs to a broken water or sewer line on your property can be costly, and standard policies usually exclude them. A service line endorsement can cover excavation and repair, up to a set limit.

## What about condo owners and tenants?

Condo owners share the risk with their condo or strata corporation. The building's master policy covers the structure, but you insure your belongings, improvements and liability. If water from your unit damages other units, the corporation may charge its deductible back to you. Our guide to [condo deductible assessments](/guides/condo-insurance-deductible-assessment-explained/) explains how to size that coverage.

Renters need their own policy, because the landlord's policy doesn't cover tenants' belongings. [Tenant insurance](/tenant-insurance/) also includes liability if your overflowing bathtub damages the unit below. If you rent in Ontario, see our guide on [whether a landlord can require tenant insurance](/guides/tenant-insurance-ontario-landlord-require/).

## How do you make a water damage claim?

Act quickly and keep records. These steps protect your claim:

1. Stop the water if it is safe to do so, and shut off the main valve.
2. Take reasonable steps to prevent more damage. Most policies require this.
3. Photograph and video the damage before cleanup begins.
4. Call your insurer or broker as soon as you can.
5. Keep receipts for emergency repairs, drying and hotel stays.
6. Don't throw out damaged items until the adjuster says you can.

If you can't resolve a dispute with your insurer, use its internal complaint process first. After that, the [General Insurance OmbudService](https://www.giocanada.org/) can help with many home insurance complaints.

## Questions to ask your insurer or broker

Bring this list to your next renewal:

- Is sudden water damage covered, and is the failed pipe or appliance covered too?
- Do I have sewer backup coverage? What are the limit and deductible?
- Is overland water available at my address, and what does it cost?
- Is groundwater or above-ground water covered or available?
- Do I have service line coverage?
- What are the freezing and vacancy conditions?
- Do backwater valves, sump pumps or water sensors earn a discount?
- For condo owners: what is my building's water deductible?
