Travel insurance with pre-existing conditions is available to many Canadians, but a condition is usually covered only if it has been stable for a set period before you leave. Insurers decide through a medical questionnaire, a stability clause and a rate category. This guide explains how each one works, what's typically asked about common conditions, and what to do if you're declined.

## Can you get travel insurance with pre-existing conditions?

Often, yes. People with a heart condition, diabetes, COPD or a cancer history can usually apply for emergency medical coverage. The condition itself is covered only if it meets the policy's stability rules: no new symptoms, medication changes, hospital stays or pending tests for a set period, such as 3 or 6 months, before departure.

There's no single definition of "pre-existing" across insurers. Each policy defines it in its own wording. The [Government of Canada's travel insurance advice](https://travel.gc.ca/travelling/documents/travel-insurance) suggests asking the insurer to explain its definition and any limits on pre-existing conditions, including the tests and treatments you've had.

Why does this matter so much? Your provincial plan pays very little once you leave the country. In Ontario, OHIP pays at most $400 a day for emergency inpatient care in an operating room, coronary care unit or intensive care unit outside Canada. It pays up to $200 a day for lower levels of care. For outpatient care, it pays the lesser of $50 a day or the amount billed, according to [Ontario's OHIP page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/ohip-coverage-while-outside-canada). OHIP also doesn't pay to move you back to an Ontario hospital for ongoing care.

So treat your provincial plan as a starting point, not a safety net. Private emergency medical coverage pays the rest, but only for conditions the policy accepts.

## How insurers decide: stability, questionnaire and rate category

Three things decide whether your condition is covered and what you pay. Each insurer uses them a little differently, so read them together.

### The stability clause

A stability clause covers a pre-existing condition only if nothing about it changed during a set window before your trip. The Government of Canada describes it as no change in the condition and no new conditions, symptoms or medications during that period.

Insurers spell out "stable" in detail. Ontario Blue Cross lists criteria such as:

- no new diagnosis
- no new symptoms or worsening of existing ones
- no hospital stay
- no new medication or change in dose
- no treatment that was prescribed or recommended but not followed

The length of the window varies by insurer, plan and age. As of our October 2026 review, [Ontario Blue Cross](https://on.bluecross.ca/travel-insurance/travel-tips/understanding-pre-existing-conditions) asks for 3 months of stability before departure for travellers aged 0 to 54. From age 55, it asks for 6 months. Alberta Blue Cross's retiree plan uses a 90-day period. Our page on [snowbird travel insurance](/travel-insurance/snowbirds/) explains how the stability period works on long winter stays.

One point surprises many travellers. [Alberta Blue Cross](https://www.ab.bluecross.ca/plans/personal/retiree/retiree-travel.php) tells its members that being medically stable to travel in a doctor's opinion is not the same as meeting its definition of stable. If you claim for a pre-existing condition, it says it will check your medical records against the stability period. So count back from your departure date, using your own records.

### The medical questionnaire

The questionnaire is how the insurer learns about your health. It asks about your conditions, medications, hospital stays and tests. Whether you need one, and from what age, depends on the insurer and plan. The Government of Canada's advice for [older travellers](https://travel.gc.ca/travelling/health-safety/older-travellers) notes that insurers may limit coverage or raise premiums or deductibles for travellers over 65.

Your answers set the terms of the policy. That makes accuracy more important than speed. A wrong or missing answer can put a claim at risk later. Have your medication list and recent appointment dates in front of you before you start. Our [travel insurance](/travel-insurance/) page covers the medical questionnaire alongside the other coverage choices.

Some questions decide eligibility outright. Manulife's [medical questionnaire for travelling Canadians](https://advisor.manulife.ca/content/dam/manulife-advisor-portal/documents/en/forms/insurance/new-business-and-underwriting/manulife-travel-insurance-medical-questionnaire-and-application-for-travelling-canadians-at0190e.pdf), for example, screens out applicants with a terminal illness or metastatic cancer, people on kidney dialysis, and people who used home oxygen in the past 12 months. Certain answers send applicants to its individually underwritten plan instead.

### The rate category

Your answers then place you in a rate category. The category sets your premium and the stability rules that apply to you. In Manulife's questionnaire, for example, Rate Category A excludes a pre-existing condition that wasn't stable in the 3 months before coverage starts.

## Heart conditions, diabetes, COPD and cancer: what's typically asked

The table below applies the published stability rules to six common conditions. It shows what questions typically focus on and what usually decides the outcome. Wording differs by insurer, so treat it as a guide to the questions, not a promise of coverage.

| Condition | What questions typically focus on | What usually decides coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Heart conditions (angina, heart attack, heart failure, irregular heartbeat) | New or changed heart medication. New or worse chest pain or shortness of breath. Hospital or emergency visits. Procedures such as a stent. Pending tests or referrals. | Covered if stable through the plan's period. A recent change can mean the condition is excluded or the rate category rises. |
| Diabetes | How it's treated (diet, pills or insulin). New prescriptions or dose changes. New complications. | Routine insulin adjustments are exempt on some plans. A newly started medication or a new complication can break stability. |
| COPD and other lung conditions | Home oxygen. New inhalers or a new prescription for a flare-up. Hospital stays. | Home oxygen in the past 12 months rules applicants out of some standard plans. Otherwise, stability decides. |
| Cancer in remission | When treatment ended. Ongoing therapy. Pending scans or results. Spread to other organs. | Finished treatment with nothing pending may meet the stability rule. Metastatic cancer or terminal illness rules applicants out of some standard plans. |
| Stroke or TIA (mini-stroke) | Date of the last event. New or changed blood thinners. New symptoms. Pending tests. | Decided by the stability rule. A new event or medication change inside the window breaks stability under typical wording. |
| High blood pressure | Medication or dose changes. New symptoms. Related hospital visits. | Controlled blood pressure follows the normal stability rule. A dose change inside the window can affect coverage. |

*Instasure summary of published policy wordings and questionnaires from Ontario Blue Cross, Alberta Blue Cross and Manulife, reviewed October 2026. Outcomes vary by insurer, plan and age, and your own policy wording decides coverage. This is general information, not medical advice.*

### Travel insurance with a heart condition

For a heart condition, insurers typically want to know about medication changes, new symptoms and any procedure or test in the stability window. If your cardiologist changed a dose shortly before your trip, the heart condition may not be covered for that trip, even if you feel well.

### Travel insurance for diabetes in Canada

If you were diagnosed before your coverage starts, diabetes counts as a pre-existing condition. The key question is whether your treatment changed. Alberta Blue Cross's retiree plan doesn't count a routine insulin adjustment not prescribed by a physician, as long as the insulin wasn't first prescribed in the 90 days before the trip. Check your own plan's wording, because exceptions differ.

### Travel insurance with COPD

Lung conditions can be screened through home oxygen use, hospital stays and new prescriptions. A flare-up that needed new medication can restart the stability clock. If you use home oxygen, expect fewer standard options and ask about individually underwritten plans.

### Travel insurance after cancer

Questions usually focus on when treatment ended and whether you're still in treatment. Many stability definitions, including Manulife's, also look for pending tests or results. If a follow-up scan or result is outstanding, ask the insurer how it treats it before you buy.

## Partial coverage and condition exclusions

A policy doesn't have to be all or nothing. Ontario Blue Cross explains that a pre-existing condition that isn't stable won't be covered, but its other coverage still applies to unrelated care. In practice, you could be covered for a broken ankle but not for a heart emergency.

Weigh that gap carefully. If the excluded condition is the one most likely to send you to hospital, the policy protects you least where you need it most. The provincial plan then pays only its capped daily amounts.

Three more points to check:

1. **Get it in writing.** The Government of Canada advises getting written confirmation that the policy covers your pre-existing condition. Without it, a claim could be denied under a pre-existing condition clause.
2. **Plan for ongoing care.** Travel medical insurance typically doesn't cover ongoing treatment for chronic conditions while you travel, the government's older-traveller advice notes. Bring enough medication and arrange refills before you leave.
3. **Check trip cancellation separately.** Trip cancellation can use a different date. Ontario Blue Cross, for example, measures stability over the 3 months before you bought the insurance or made your first trip payment, whichever came later.

## Options if you're declined

A decline from one insurer isn't the final word. Questionnaires, stability periods and rate categories differ, so here's where to look next:

- **Another insurer's standard plan.** A 3-month window with one insurer may fit your history where a 6-month window with another does not.
- **A shorter stability option.** Ontario Blue Cross offers a Reduced Stability Period option for a shorter window.
- **An individually underwritten plan.** Some insurers, Manulife among them, review your health history case by case when the standard questionnaire doesn't fit.
- **Waiting until you're stable.** If a recent medication change broke stability, a later departure date may let the condition meet the rule.
- **A licensed advisor's review.** An advisor can match your history to each insurer's wording before you apply.

Don't travel on a policy you don't understand. Ask which conditions are covered, which are excluded, and get the answer in writing.

## Getting coverage reviewed before departure

If you're buying travel insurance with pre-existing conditions, use this checklist a few weeks before you leave, and again in the days before departure:

1. **List every condition and medication.** Note the date of each change, test, referral and hospital visit in the past year.
2. **Find your stability period.** Check it for your age and plan, then count back from your departure date.
3. **Answer the questionnaire from your records.** If you're unsure of a date, check with your doctor's office first.
4. **Ask for written confirmation.** Confirm each pre-existing condition is covered, or exactly what's excluded.
5. **Report changes before you leave.** If a dose changes or a new symptom appears after you buy, call the insurer before departure.

If you live in Ontario, the OHIP limits above are what you fall back on without private coverage. Ontario notes that emergency care in other countries costs more than OHIP may pay, and strongly advises private travel insurance. ## Michael's take

As a planner, I look at travel insurance for a pre-existing condition the way I look at any risk that could undo years of saving. A higher premium is a cost you can budget for. An unpaid hospital bill abroad is not. My rule of thumb is to put the questionnaire on the calendar like a tax deadline. Give it time, gather the records, and don't rush it at the airport.

I'd also plan the trip around your health, not the other way round. If your doctor is likely to adjust a medication, try to do it well before the stability window starts. And if the only policy you can find excludes the condition you worry about most, treat that as a reason to talk to an advisor before you book, not after.
