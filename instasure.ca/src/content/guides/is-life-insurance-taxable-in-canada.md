Is life insurance taxable in Canada? In most cases, no: the death benefit your beneficiary receives is not taxed. Tax shows up around the edges instead. It applies when you cash in or borrow against a permanent policy, when interest is added to a payout, and when an employer or a corporation is involved. This guide walks through each situation with the federal source behind it.

## Is life insurance taxable in Canada? The short answer

Generally, no. The Canada Revenue Agency (CRA) lists most amounts received from a life insurance policy after someone's death as income that is not reported or taxed. Tax can apply to interest added to a delayed payout, to cash value you take out above the policy's cost, and to premiums your employer pays. Premiums on a personal policy are not deductible.

## Are life insurance payouts taxable in Canada for beneficiaries?

When you name a beneficiary, the insurer pays that person directly. The Financial Consumer Agency of Canada (FCAC) describes the death benefit as a one-time, tax-free payment. The CRA's list of amounts that are not reported or taxed includes most amounts received from a life insurance policy after a death. So your beneficiary doesn't add the payout to their income.

The CRA rule isn't limited to one type of coverage. It covers [term life insurance](/term-life-insurance/) and permanent policies alike, including a small [funeral insurance](/life-insurance/funeral-expense/) policy.

Two things can still create tax for the beneficiary:

- **Interest on a delayed payout.** Sun Life notes that interest that builds up between the date of death and the payment date is taxable. The beneficiary reports it as investment income.
- **What the money earns later.** Once the payout is invested, the interest, dividends or gains it earns are taxed like any other investment income.

### Name a beneficiary, and a backup

FCAC warns that if you don't name a beneficiary, your insurer will assume your estate is the beneficiary. It also suggests naming a contingent beneficiary. That person receives the money if your first beneficiary dies before you or at the same time. Check your designations after a marriage, a separation or a new child.

## When the payout goes to your estate

Paying the estate doesn't turn the death benefit into taxable income. It does change where the money goes. According to FCAC, a death benefit paid to your estate becomes part of the estate and is distributed under your will. It can be subject to estate taxes, and creditors may claim it to pay your outstanding debts.

That is the core of the question "does life insurance go through probate?" With a named beneficiary, the payout generally stays out of the estate. Paid to the estate, it is counted with everything else.

**An Ontario example.** Ontario's [Estate Administration Tax](https://www.ontario.ca/page/estate-administration-tax) is based on the value of the estate. Ontario counts insurance only when the proceeds are left to the estate, so a policy that pays a named beneficiary is left out. There's no tax on an estate of $50,000 or less. Above that, the rate is $15 for every $1,000, or part of $1,000, over $50,000.

So for an estate already worth more than $50,000, a $500,000 payout to the estate would add $7,500 of Estate Administration Tax (500 × $15). Each province sets its own probate process and fees. For Ontario coverage options, see [life insurance in Ontario](/life-insurance/ontario/).

### How the CPP death benefit is taxed

The CPP death benefit is a government benefit, not insurance, and it is taxed differently. Under the [federal CPP rules](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp/cpp-death-benefit.html), it is a one-time payment of $2,500. For deaths on or after January 1, 2025, an extra $2,500 can be paid to the estate of a contributor who dies before collecting a CPP retirement or disability pension and leaves no survivor.

The CRA says the CPP death benefit is generally reported by the estate on a T3 return. A beneficiary who receives it reports it on their own return instead. It isn't eligible for the $10,000 exemption that applies to death benefits paid by an employer.

## Is cash surrender value taxable? Cash value, surrenders and policy loans

This section matters if you own a permanent policy. FCAC notes that permanent policies usually build up a cash value. If you cancel, you get that value back, usually less than you paid in premiums. That cash value is why [whole life insurance](/whole-life-insurance/) costs far more than term.

For example, Instasure's estimate for a 40-year-old male non-smoker in Ontario, as of October 2026, is about $225 to $319.50 a month for $250,000 of participating whole life. The same person's estimate for $500,000 of 20-year term is about $36 to $51.50 a month. These are Instasure estimates for an example profile, not quotes. Our [term vs whole life insurance](/guides/term-vs-whole-life-insurance-canada/) guide explains when each fits.

### The key number: your adjusted cost basis

Section 148 of the Income Tax Act defines the policy's adjusted cost basis (ACB). It is roughly what you've paid into the policy, adjusted for certain items. Premiums you pay generally increase it. Income you've already been taxed on is also added, so it isn't taxed twice. Your insurer can tell you the current ACB.

### Surrendering a policy

The Act treats a surrender as a "disposition" of the policy. You add to your income the amount by which the cash you receive exceeds the ACB, according to CRA bulletin IT-87R2. The gain is income for the year, not a capital gain.

Here's a hypothetical example. You surrender a policy with $40,000 of cash value and an ACB of $25,000. You'd add $15,000 to your income for that year. A partial withdrawal is also a disposition, but only a share of the ACB is used in the calculation.

### Life insurance policy loan tax

The Act's definition of a disposition includes a policy loan. If the loan is larger than the ACB, the difference is taxable income in the year you borrow. A loan that stays within the ACB isn't taxed at that point. FCAC adds that an unpaid loan can reduce the amount your beneficiary receives.

Interest on a policy loan is deductible only in limited cases. Under CRA's [Income Tax Folio S3-F6-C1](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/technical-information/income-tax/income-tax-folios-index/series-3-property-investments-savings-plans/series-3-property-investments-savings-plan-folio-6-interest/income-tax-folio-s3-f6-c1-interest-deductibility.html), you must use the money to earn business or investment income. The insurer must also verify the interest on Form T2210 by your filing due date.

### Policy dividends and growth inside the policy

Participating policies can pay policy dividends. IT-87R2 explains that a dividend paid to you is treated as a disposition. The part applied automatically to premiums or to repay a policy loan is excluded. Dividends become taxable only when they add up to more than the ACB in a year.

Growth inside the policy depends on whether it is an exempt policy. IT-87R2 describes an exempt policy as one issued mainly for protection, not investment. Its savings can't exceed those of benchmark "exemption test policies." Growth in an exempt policy isn't taxed each year. A non-exempt policy's income is taxed every year as it accrues. Ask your insurer which kind you own.

## Are life insurance premiums tax deductible?

For a personal policy, no. The CRA says you can't deduct your life insurance premiums in most cases. You pay them from after-tax income.

There are three situations where tax and premiums meet:

1. **A policy assigned as collateral.** If a lender requires your policy as collateral for a business or investment loan, part of the premium may be deductible. CRA bulletin IT-309R2 caps it at the lesser of the premium and the policy's net cost of pure insurance. Only the part related to the loan balance counts. The lender must be a "restricted financial institution," and you must own the policy. In CRA's example, $500,000 of coverage secures a $200,000 loan, so 40% of that amount qualifies.
2. **Group life through work.** Premiums your employer pays for group term life are a taxable benefit, according to CRA guide T4130. They're added to your T4 income. Amounts you pay toward the coverage yourself reduce the benefit.
3. **A gift to charity.** CRA's guide [Gifts and income tax](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/forms-publications/publications/p113/p113-gifts-income-tax.html) explains that when you name a qualified donee, such as a registered charity, as beneficiary, the payout can be claimed as a gift on your final return. Income limits apply.

## Corporate life insurance tax: corporate-owned policies

Many business owners hold coverage inside their corporation, for example to protect against the loss of a key person or to fund a shareholder agreement. Our page on [corporate-owned life insurance for key people and buy-sell agreements](/business-insurance/key-person/) covers the coverage side. Here is the tax side.

**The capital dividend account (CDA).** CRA's Folio S3-F2-C1 explains that when a private corporation receives life insurance proceeds on a death, it adds the proceeds minus the policy's ACB to its CDA. For deaths since March 22, 2016, the ACB used is the policyholder's ACB just before the death. This applies even when the corporation was not the policyholder.

**Paying it out.** The corporation can elect to pay capital dividends out of the CDA. It files Form T2054 by the earlier of the day the dividend becomes payable and the first day any part is paid. Shareholders resident in Canada don't include the capital dividend in their income. If the election exceeds the CDA balance, the corporation can owe Part III tax on the excess.

**Policies assigned to a lender.** The folio adds that if a policy is assigned as collateral and the debtor remains entitled to the proceeds as beneficiary or policyholder, the net proceeds still go to the debtor's CDA.

Ownership, beneficiary and premium payer all matter here. Set them up with your accountant before the policy is issued, not after.

## Michael's take

As a planner, the first thing I check isn't a tax rule. It's the beneficiary designation. The death benefit is tax-free either way, but naming a person, plus a backup, keeps the money out of the estate. In Ontario, that keeps it out of the Estate Administration Tax calculation and away from creditors of the estate. My rule of thumb is to review designations at every major life event, the same way you'd review your will.

With permanent coverage, I treat cash value as tax-deferred, not tax-free. Before you surrender, borrow or switch a policy, ask your insurer for the ACB and the taxable amount in writing, and run it past your accountant. And I'd buy coverage for the protection it gives your plan. The tax treatment is a useful feature of a well-built plan, not the reason to buy a policy.
