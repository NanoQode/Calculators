Trucking insurance in Canada has no standard price. What you pay depends on what you haul, how far you drive, who is behind the wheel and whose operating authority you run under. This guide explains the coverages, the filings regulators require and the factors insurers price, so you can see where your own premium comes from.

## How much does trucking insurance cost in Canada?

There is no reliable average for trucking insurance cost in Canada. We found no regulator or industry body that publishes typical truck premiums, and quotes swing widely with cargo, radius, driver records and claims. A leased owner-operator buys fewer coverages than a carrier on its own authority. So this guide explains the price drivers instead of quoting a range.

One figure shows how hard some trucks are to place. Facility Association runs the residual market, which makes auto insurance available to owners who can't get it from a regular insurer. Its President and CEO reported that its residual market premium volume in 2023 was over $530 million. Commercial and interurban (long-haul) trucking made up $287 million of that, or more than half ([Facility Association AGM remarks](https://www.facilityassociation.com/docs/AGM%20Remarks%20of%20the%20President%20&%20CEO.pdf)).

## Four trucking profiles and what each one needs

The table compares four common set-ups. It summarizes coverage and filings, not price, because only an insurer can rate a premium.

| Profile (example) | Coverage usually placed | Filings and certificates | What pushes the price up |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owner-operator leased to a carrier | Physical damage on your own truck. Liability and cargo depend on the lease. | You run under the carrier's CVOR or safety certificate. | Lease chargebacks. Your driving record. Gaps when you're off dispatch. |
| Local dump truck on its own authority, one province | Liability, accident benefits, physical damage and general liability for the yard. | Your own CVOR in Ontario, or your province's safety certificate. | Heavy loads. Job-site exposure. A short claims history. |
| Regional dry van, for-hire, crossing provinces | Liability, physical damage, motor truck cargo and trailer coverage. | A safety fitness certificate under the federal rules for extra-provincial carriers. | Kilometres driven. Cargo value and theft. Driver turnover. |
| New authority in its first year, possibly cross-border | The full for-hire package, plus US filings if you cross the border. | A new CVOR or safety certificate. FMCSA authority and insurer filings for US loads. | No loss history. Newer drivers. US exposure. |

*Instasure summary as of October 2026, for illustration only. It is not a quote or price estimate. Your needs depend on your lease, contracts, cargo and province.*

## Insurer submission checklist

A complete submission gets a faster, more accurate answer. Gather these before you ask for commercial truck insurance quotes:

- **Authority numbers.** Your CVOR or National Safety Code certificate, plus USDOT and MC numbers if you haul into the US.
- **Carrier abstract.** In Ontario, the Level 2 [CVOR abstract](https://www.ontario.ca/page/get-commercial-vehicle-operators-registration-cvor-abstract-or-carrier-record) shows five years of collisions, convictions and inspections, and only the carrier can order it.
- **Drivers.** Licence class, years on the same type of truck, and a current abstract for each.
- **Units.** Year, make, VIN, value and owner or lender for every tractor and trailer.
- **Commodities.** What you haul, the share of each, and the highest value on a single load.
- **Radius and lanes.** Local, provincial, interprovincial or named US states.
- **Loss runs.** Claims history from your previous insurers, ideally for five years.
- **Contracts.** Your lease and shipper or broker agreements, with the limits each requires.
- **Safety program.** Hiring standards, training, maintenance records and any telematics or cameras.
- **Parking.** Where trucks and loaded trailers sit overnight and how they're secured.

## What coverages does a trucking operation need?

### Auto liability: the legal floor

Every truck needs third-party liability coverage. Ontario requires each vehicle under a CVOR to be insured under a commercial auto liability policy with the minimum coverages its loads call for, such as dangerous goods ([Ontario CVOR page](https://www.ontario.ca/page/commercial-vehicle-operators-registration-cvor)).

Carriers that cross provincial lines face a federal floor. Under the federal [Motor Carrier Safety Fitness Certificate Regulations](https://laws-lois.justice.gc.ca/eng/regulations/SOR-2005-180/), a province can't issue a safety fitness certificate to an extra-provincial truck operation without proof of at least $1,000,000 of liability coverage per vehicle. The minimum is $2,000,000 for each vehicle that carries dangerous goods. Contracts often ask for more, so check yours.

### Physical damage

Collision and comprehensive coverage pay to repair or replace your own tractor and trailers. If your truck is financed or leased, check what the lender or lessor requires before you choose a deductible.

### Motor truck cargo

Cargo insurance in Canada pays for loss or damage to goods you carry for others, for example after theft, a collision or a fire. Read the exclusions closely. Refrigeration breakdown may need an extension, and theft from an unattended truck can carry conditions. Match your cargo limit to the most valuable load you haul, not the average one.

### General liability and other pieces

[General liability insurance](/business-insurance/) covers injury and property damage from operations off the road, such as a yard or loading dock. Ask about trailer interchange if you pull trailers you don't own. For property, cyber and liability pricing beyond the trucks, see our [small business insurance cost](/guides/small-business-insurance-cost-canada/) guide.

## Owner-operators leased to a carrier vs your own authority

This is the biggest fork in owner operator truck insurance cost, because it decides which policies are yours.

### Leased to a carrier

When you lease on, you usually haul under the carrier's authority. In Ontario, the operator responsible for a truck doesn't have to own it. Proof of the lease must ride in the truck, and the lease must name the vehicle and the lessee's CVOR number if the certificate isn't carried. Before you sign, ask:

1. Whose liability and cargo policy covers me while I'm under dispatch?
2. Am I covered when I drive without a load or off dispatch?
3. Who pays the deductible after a cargo or collision claim, and can it be charged back to me?
4. Must I carry physical damage, and must the carrier be named on it?

Your bill covers fewer pieces than a carrier's, but lease chargebacks are part of your real insurance cost.

### Running your own authority

With your own CVOR or safety certificate, you are the carrier. You buy liability, cargo and physical damage yourself, and every claim and inspection builds your own record. For-hire trucking insurance on your own authority is the full [commercial auto insurance](/business-insurance/commercial-auto/) package.

### Hauling into the United States

The [Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration](https://www.fmcsa.dot.gov/registration/insurance-filing-requirements) requires Canadian-based for-hire carriers of regulated goods to hold US operating authority. FMCSA won't grant it until proof of insurance and a BOC-3 process-agent form are on file. Your insurer, not you, files the proof on Form BMC-91 or BMC-91X. As FMCSA listed them in October 2026, the minimums are:

- $750,000 for for-hire carriers of non-hazardous property in vehicles rated 10,001 pounds or more.
- $1,000,000 for for-hire carriers of certain hazardous materials.
- $5,000,000 for carriers of explosives, poison gas or radioactive materials.

FMCSA requires cargo insurance filings from household goods movers, not general freight carriers, though your contracts may still demand cargo coverage. Insurance Bureau of Canada (IBC) also says premiums for trucks travelling outside Canada reflect exposure to excessive US jury verdicts.

## What drives the price of trucking insurance?

### What you haul

In an April 2024 report IBC commissioned from MNP, collisions involving heavy loads or dangerous goods tend to be more severe. The required liability limit also rises for those loads, which raises premiums ([IBC](https://www.ibc.ca/news-insights/news/urgent-action-needed-for-commercial-truck-driver-training)).

### Where and how far you drive

More kilometres means more exposure, and US lanes add lawsuit risk. Where your trucks park matters too. Équité Association's June 2024 report found that thefts of trucks, trailers and cargo loads from 2019 to 2023 were worth an estimated $531 million or more. Over $239 million of stolen cargo and equipment remained unrecovered, and the average recovered cargo load was worth $114,000 ([Équité Association](https://www.equiteassociation.com/press-releases/equite-association-releases-its-inaugural-cargo-and-heavy-equipment-theft-trend-report)).

### Who drives

The same IBC report found that drivers with less training and experience are more likely to crash and make costly claims. Truck drivers with less than three years of experience had a greater likelihood of a collision. Training, hours behind the wheel and claims history are among the measures insurers review.

### Your safety rating

Ontario gives carriers one of five CVOR safety ratings: Excellent, Satisfactory, Satisfactory–unaudited, Conditional and Unsatisfactory. A carrier can drop to Conditional if its on-road performance passes 70% of its CVOR threshold or it fails a facility audit. It can be rated Unsatisfactory if its overall violation rate goes over its threshold. Insurers can ask for your CVOR abstract, so violations can follow you to renewal.

## New ventures and drivers with tickets

A new authority has no loss history, so insurers price it with less information. In Ontario, a new operator's proposed rating is Satisfactory–unaudited, and most stay there for their first six months, according to Ontario's [CVOR and carrier safety reference guide](https://www.ontario.ca/files/2026-04/mto-cvor-program-carrier-safety-module-reference-guide-en-2026-04-20.pdf) (April 2026). An Excellent rating needs at least 24 months of operation in Ontario and an excellent score in a ministry facility audit.

IBC's 2024 report also concluded that entry-level training alone doesn't fully prepare drivers for a heavy truck in all conditions. It called for onboarding and mentorship, and for piloting telematics feedback to drivers.

Drivers with tickets or at-fault collisions narrow your choice of insurers. Never leave a driver off the list, because an undisclosed driver can put a claim at risk. Where the regular market says no, Facility Association's residual market is the fallback in the provinces it serves.

## Truck insurance in Ontario: CVOR and the Peel corridor

Truck insurance in Ontario starts with the CVOR. Trucks with a registered gross weight or actual weight over 4,500 kg need a CVOR certificate, as do buses that seat 10 or more passengers and tow trucks.

Location matters here too. Équité's 2024 report named Mississauga, Brampton, Vaughan, Toronto and Montreal as the top five cargo theft hotspots. It added that Mississauga and Brampton have some of the highest losses in North America. If your yard is in Peel, see our pages on [business insurance in Brampton](/business-insurance/ontario/brampton/) and [Mississauga](/business-insurance/ontario/mississauga/). For the wider province, start with [business insurance in Ontario](/business-insurance/ontario/) or [business insurance in Toronto](/business-insurance/ontario/toronto/).

One Ontario lever is easy to misuse. Since January 2024, the OPCF 49 endorsement lets you opt out of Direct Compensation – Property Damage coverage. The [Financial Services Regulatory Authority of Ontario](https://www.fsrao.ca/consumers/auto-insurance/purchasing-your-policy/what-standard-auto-insurance-policy) warns that if you opt out, your insurer can't offer you collision or upset coverage. Weigh that trade-off carefully if your truck is financed.

## Alberta and other provinces

Alberta carriers operating only in Alberta need a safety fitness certificate for trucks, tractors or trailers registered at 11,794 kg or more. Alberta's [Commercial Vehicle Certificate and Insurance Regulation](https://kings-printer.alberta.ca/documents/Regs/2002_314.pdf) sets minimum liability coverage for carriers at $1 million, or $2 million for certain dangerous goods. See our page on [business insurance in Alberta](/business-insurance/alberta/) for local options.

Carriers anywhere in Canada that cross provincial lines need a safety fitness certificate from their home province for trucks over 4,500 kg, and the federal minimums above apply. Provinces also run programs for local carriers. British Columbia, for example, requires a [National Safety Code certificate](https://www.cvse.ca/national_safety_code.htm) for commercial vehicles with BC plates.

## How to lower trucking insurance premiums

Saving money shouldn't mean leaving big losses uncovered.

1. **Hire for experience.** Drivers with more years on the same class of truck present less risk.
2. **Document your safety program.** Put hiring, training, maintenance and onboarding standards in writing.
3. **Use telematics.** Driver feedback data you already collect can support your file.
4. **Describe your operation accurately.** A wrong commodity or radius can lead to a denied claim.
5. **Protect your rating.** Fix the causes of inspection failures and convictions before your renewal.
6. **Cut theft exposure.** Use secure yards, and don't leave loaded trailers unattended for long periods.
7. **Choose deductibles you can fund.** A higher deductible lowers the premium only if you can pay it after a claim.
8. **Work with a specialist.** Trucks are placed with insurers that write commercial risks, so access to those markets matters.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I look at trucking insurance the way I look at any fixed cost that could sink a small business. It has to fit your cash flow, and it has to cover the loss you couldn't survive. My rule of thumb is to add up your worst realistic month: a cargo deductible, a repair deductible and any lease chargeback. Hold that amount in a separate reserve before you chase a lower premium with higher deductibles.

For owner-operators, I'd look past the truck as well. Your income depends on your health and your licence, and if you have no group plan behind you, a long injury can stop everything. Once the truck and cargo are covered, the next step is [disability insurance for the self-employed](/disability-insurance/self-employed/) and enough life insurance to clear the truck loan. Buy what protects the plan, not what is sold hardest.
