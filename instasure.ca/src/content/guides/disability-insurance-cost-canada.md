Disability insurance cost in Canada depends mostly on your age, your job and how long the policy would pay. For a 40-year-old non-smoking man in a professional job with a $5,000 monthly benefit, Instasure's model estimates $140 to $218 a month, as of October 2026. Below you'll find a dated table by age, sex and occupation class, and the levers that move your own premium.

## What does disability insurance cost in Canada? (Quick answer)

For a $5,000 monthly benefit with a 90-day waiting period and benefits to age 65, Instasure's model estimates a typical $110 to $234 a month for non-smoking men aged 30 to 50 in office or professional jobs. Our model puts women, smokers and the skilled trades higher. These are estimates as of October 2026, not quotes.

There's no single average price, because each insurer sets its own rates, usually by occupational class and age. The benefit amount, waiting period, benefit period and definition of disability matter too. Income protection insurance cost follows the same rules.

Three patterns stand out:

- **Age.** The typical estimate rises by about a third from 30 to 40, and again from 40 to 50.
- **Job.** Skilled trades come in at about 1.7 times the professional estimate.
- **Sex.** Our model's estimate for a woman is about a quarter higher than for a man in the same job. Insurers set their own rate tables, so compare real quotes.

Our model sizes the benefit at about 65% of gross income, which is why a $92,000 income gives a $5,000 benefit.

## Waiting period and benefit period: two big price levers

The waiting period, or elimination period, is the time between the start of a disability and your first payment. The benefit period is how long payments can last. Both are choices you make when you apply, and the options differ by insurer and plan. A longer wait or a shorter benefit period lowers the premium; a licensed advisor can show you which combinations each insurer offers.

### The waiting period

Our model prices only a 90-day wait, so it can't show the dollar difference for 30 or 120 days. With a longer wait, the insurer pays nothing on shorter absences and fewer months on long ones, so the premium is generally lower. Ask a licensed advisor to quote 30, 90 and 120 days side by side.

A longer wait only works if something covers those months. Sick leave from work is one option. [EI sickness benefits](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/ei/ei-sickness.html) are another: they can pay up to 26 weeks on claims established on or after December 18, 2022. You generally need 600 insurable hours in the 52 weeks before your claim. The [benefit rate](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/ei/ei-sickness/benefit-amount.html) is 55% of your insurable weekly earnings, up to $729 a week for claims starting on or after December 28, 2025. That's about $3,160 a month at most.

### The benefit period

In our model, the 40-year-old professional man drops from $176 a month to $96.80 with a short benefit period of two to five years. That's about 45% less.

The trade-off is large. On a to-65 policy, a claim that began at 40 and lasted to 65 could pay close to $1.5 million on the $5,000 benefit. A two-year benefit period pays at most $120,000, and a five-year period at most $300,000. That's why long term disability insurance cost is mostly a question of how long you need the income to last.

## Own-occupation and riders: what they add

How a policy defines "disabled" decides when it pays. Some policies start with a test based on your own or regular occupation, then switch to a broader test. In RBC Insurance's [sample Fundamental Series policy](https://www.rbcinsurance.com/health-insurance/pdf/fundamental-series-sample-policy.pdf), total disability means you can't do your regular occupation for the first 36 months. After that, the test is whether you can do any reasonable occupation that fits your education, training or experience.

In our model, an own-occupation definition plus a cost-of-living rider raises the 40-year-old example from $176 to $238 a month, about 35% more. The add-ons to ask about:

- **Own-occupation definition.** Pays if you can't do your own job, even if you could do another.
- **Cost-of-living rider.** Indexes your benefit to inflation while you're on claim.
- **Future insurability option.** Lets you raise coverage as your income grows.
- **Premium waiver.** Ask whether premiums stop while you're on claim.

If you're weighing a lump sum against monthly income, our guide to [critical illness vs disability insurance](/guides/critical-illness-vs-disability-insurance/) explains how the two work together.

Insurers sort jobs into occupation classes, and each class has its own rates. That's why the disability insurance premium by occupation can vary so widely. For the 40-year-old non-smoking man with a $5,000 benefit, here's how our model's typical estimate changes by class:

Heavy manual work comes in at more than two and a half times the office estimate. Real insurers use their own class names and rules, so describe your actual duties on the application, not just your title.

### Self-employed and gig workers

If you work for yourself, there's usually no group plan behind you. You can get EI sickness benefits only if you [register for EI special benefits](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/ei/ei-self-employed-workers/eligibility.html) and wait 12 months after your registration is confirmed. Insurers usually confirm your income from tax returns.

| Change from the example profile | Typical monthly estimate |
|---|---|

| Smoker | $228 |
| $3,250 benefit ($60,000 income) | $114 |
| $6,500 benefit ($120,000 income) | $228 |
| Benefit period of two to five years | $96.80 |
| Own-occupation definition plus cost-of-living rider | $238 |

*Instasure estimates, as of October 2026. Real insurers weigh these factors differently.*

The levers worth pulling first:

1. **Size the benefit to your fixed costs**, such as housing, debt payments and food, rather than every dollar you earn.
2. **Stretch the waiting period** to 90 or 120 days, but only if your savings can bridge it.
3. **Buy earlier.** In our model, the same cover costs about a third more at 40 than at 30. Ask whether the premium is level or rises with age.
4. **Stop smoking.** Our model charges a smoker about 30% more.
5. **Choose riders on purpose**, and keep the long benefit period that protects you from a long claim.

## Group vs individual cost

Group long term disability insurance through work often costs you less out of pocket, because your employer may pay some or all of it. The trade-offs are tax, portability and fit.

| Feature | Group long-term disability | Individual disability insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Who pays | Your employer pays some or all, or you pay through payroll | You |
| Tax on benefits | Generally taxable if your employer contributed to the plan | Not taxable when you pay the entire cost |
| If you change jobs | Tied to that employer | Stays with you |
| Benefit and definition | Set by the plan for everyone in it | Chosen by you, within the insurer's limits |

The tax rule comes from the [Canada Revenue Agency](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/about-your-tax-return/tax-return/completing-a-tax-return/personal-income/line-10400-other-employment-income.html). Payments from a sickness, accident, disability or income maintenance plan are reported as income. If you pay the entire cost of the plan, the amounts you receive are not taxable.

The CPP disability benefit sits under both. The [maximum for 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/publicpensions/cpp-disability-benefit/benefit-amount.html) is $1,741.20 a month, about a third of the example $5,000 benefit. Your disability must be severe and prolonged, and the benefit ends at 65. Severe means you're regularly unable to do any substantially gainful work, which the [CPP disability toolkit](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/programs/pension-plan-disability-benefits/reports/toolkit.html) sets at earnings of $20,971.45 a year in 2026.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I see disability insurance as protection for the asset that pays for everything else in the plan: your ability to earn. My rule of thumb is to size the benefit around your fixed costs first, such as housing, debt payments and retirement savings, not around your gross salary. If the benefit covers those, the rest of the plan can survive a long claim.

Where I'd look for savings is the waiting period, not the benefit period. A 90- or 120-day wait backed by an emergency fund is a trade I'm comfortable with. A short benefit period saves more on paper, but it removes cover for the long claim that would actually derail a retirement plan. If you're self-employed, I'd sort out disability cover before most other insurance, because there's no employer plan to replace your pay.
