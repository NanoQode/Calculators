Canadian Dental Care Plan eligibility comes down to four tests. You must be a resident of Canada for tax purposes and have filed last year's tax return. Your adjusted family net income must be under $90,000, and you can't have access to private dental insurance. Pass all four and you can apply online or by phone. Below are each test, the edge cases and your options if you don't qualify.

## Canadian Dental Care Plan eligibility: who qualifies?

You qualify for the Canadian Dental Care Plan (CDCP) if you meet all four requirements on [canada.ca](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/dental/dental-care-plan/qualify.html). You're a resident of Canada for tax purposes. You filed a tax return in Canada, and so did your spouse or common-law partner. Your adjusted family net income is less than $90,000. And you have no access to private dental insurance.

As of October 2026, every eligible age group can apply. Seniors came first. Children under 18 and adults with a valid Disability Tax Credit certificate followed in June 2024. [Adults aged 18 to 64](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/news/2025/03/canadian-dental-care-plan-expands-to-include-millions-of-new-eligible-canadians.html) were the last group added, in May 2025.

Applications are open for the 2026–2027 benefit year, which runs from July 1, 2026, to June 30, 2027. There's no premium. Depending on your income, you may pay a co-payment at the dentist.

## CDCP eligibility flowchart (October 2026)

Work through these four questions in order. A "no" at step 1, 2 or 3, or a "yes" at step 4, ends the check.

| Step | Question | If yes | If no |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Residency | Are you a resident of Canada for tax purposes? | Go to step 2 | Not eligible |
| 2. Tax filing | Did you file last year's tax return in Canada, and did your spouse or common-law partner file theirs? | Go to step 3 | Not eligible until the returns are filed |
| 3. Income | Is your adjusted family net income less than $90,000? | Go to step 4 | Not eligible at $90,000 or more |
| 4. Access | Can you get private dental coverage through work, a pension, a family member's plan, an association, a health spending account or a plan you bought? | Not eligible (one exception for some retirees, below) | You pass all four tests: apply |

*Source: Government of Canada, "Do you qualify – Canadian Dental Care Plan", reviewed October 2026. The rules can change, so confirm on canada.ca before you apply.*

## The income test: the CDCP income limit

The CDCP income limit is an adjusted family net income of less than $90,000. "Less than" matters: a family at exactly $90,000 doesn't qualify. "Family" means you plus your spouse or common-law partner, if you have one.

The figure starts from net income on line 23600 of each return. It is then adjusted for Universal Child Care Benefit and Registered Disability Savings Plan amounts. You don't calculate it yourself. The Canada Revenue Agency works it out from your returns and tells Service Canada whether you meet the threshold.

Your income also sets your co-payment, according to the [CDCP coverage page](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/dental/dental-care-plan/coverage.html):

| Adjusted family net income | Your co-payment | The CDCP pays |
|---|---|---|
| Less than $70,000 | None | 100% of the CDCP fee |
| $70,000 to $79,999 | 40% of the CDCP fee | 60% of the CDCP fee |
| $80,000 to $89,999 | 60% of the CDCP fee | 40% of the CDCP fee |
| $90,000 or more | Not eligible | Nothing |

Canada.ca's own examples: a family with $76,000 pays a 40% co-payment, and one with $82,000 pays 60%. A person with $32,000 pays none. Co-payments aren't the only cost, though. You may pay extra charges in some situations, so ask your dental office what you'll owe before treatment starts.

Which year counts? Members who renewed for the 2026–2027 benefit year needed their 2025 Notice of Assessment from the CRA.

## What "access to dental insurance" means for CDCP eligibility

This is the test people most often get wrong. It looks at whether private dental coverage is available to you, not whether you use it. Canada.ca says you're not eligible even if you've never used the coverage, decided not to enrol or would have to pay a premium for it.

| Your situation | Counts as access? | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Your employer offers dental and you declined or opted out | Yes | Not eligible |
| You have workplace or pension dental you've never used | Yes | Not eligible |
| You'd have to pay a premium for the workplace or pension plan | Yes | Not eligible |
| You can be covered through a family member's work or pension plan | Yes | Not eligible |
| A health spending account that covers dental costs | Yes | Not eligible |
| Dental coverage through a professional or student association | Yes | Not eligible |
| A private plan you bought that includes dental | Yes | Not eligible |
| You retired, opted out of pension dental before December 11, 2023, and can't opt back in | Exception | You may be eligible |
| Dental coverage through a government social program | Not private coverage | List it on your application |

Your spouse's details on your application are also used to check for dental coverage through them. A plan through your partner's job can therefore rule you out.

[Employers and pension plan administrators](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/dental/dental-care-plan/reporting-access.html) now report whether dental coverage was offered, in box 45 of your T4 slip or box 015 of a T4A. They report the offer, not whether you took it.

The CDCP relies on your own attestation, and Service Canada can [review your eligibility](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/dental/dental-care-plan/member-eligibility-review.html) after you're enrolled. If you're selected, you get a letter and have 45 days from its issue date to respond. If you're found ineligible, you may have to repay what the plan paid for you or your dependants.

> **Tip:** If you want a private health plan for drugs or physiotherapy, check whether it includes dental before you buy. Our guide to [private dental insurance vs the CDCP](/guides/private-dental-insurance-vs-cdcp/) explains when a health-only plan may fit alongside the CDCP.

## Residency and tax-filing requirements

You must be a resident of Canada for tax purposes. You must also have filed a tax return in Canada for the previous year. If you have a spouse or common-law partner, they must have filed too. File even if your income was low, because the CRA needs the returns to calculate your adjusted family net income.

Two family rules catch people out:

- **Your spouse's return doesn't enrol them.** Their details on your application confirm your marital status, family income and dental coverage. Canada.ca says your spouse or common-law partner must submit their own application to be covered.
- **Children go on your application.** You give details for each dependant, including a Social Insurance Number if the child has one.

Eligibility is confirmed each year, so you renew every spring once your Notice of Assessment arrives.

## How to apply for the Canadian Dental Care Plan

The [CDCP apply page](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/dental/dental-care-plan/apply.html) lists two routes: online or by phone. Here are the steps.

1. **Check the four tests** in the flowchart above. Stop if you have access to any private dental plan.
2. **Gather your details.** For each applicant you need a full name, date of birth, home and mailing address and Social Insurance Number (for children, if available). Add any dental coverage through government social programs, plus your spouse's or common-law partner's information.
3. **Apply online.** Sign in to My Service Canada Account or create a profile. Open the Canadian Dental Care Plan section on your dashboard and choose the option to apply.
4. **Or apply by phone.** If you can't use My Service Canada Account, call 1-833-537-4342 (TTY 1-833-677-6262). Help with applying is available Monday to Friday, 8:30 a.m. to 4:30 p.m. local time.
5. **Have your spouse apply.** If your spouse or common-law partner wants coverage, they submit their own application.
6. **Watch for your letter.** Don't book care until you know your coverage start date.

## After you apply: enrolment, cards and finding a dentist

Service Canada sends a letter by mail or email, depending on your preference, confirming whether you're enrolled. If you are, it lists your member ID, coverage start date and co-payment level. You can also see it in My Service Canada Account. Once you're enrolled, Sun Life, which administers the plan for the federal government, mails a [welcome package](https://www.canada.ca/en/services/benefits/dental/dental-care-plan/after-apply.html) with your member card.

Book appointments only on or after your coverage start date, because care before that date isn't covered. Canada.ca also advises confirming that your coverage is active before every appointment. Your Sun Life online account shows your plan details and claims history, and lets you print a member card.

Since July 2024, all dental care providers can provide care under the CDCP. Still, ask the office before you book whether it sees CDCP patients.

Then put the renewal in your calendar. The [2026 renewal window](https://www.canada.ca/en/employment-social-development/news/2026/04/canadian-dental-care-plan-renewal-season-opens-april-15.html) ran from April 15 to June 1, 2026. Renewals for 2027–2028 open in spring 2027. If you miss the deadline, you can apply again, but care during the gap isn't covered or reimbursed later. Health Canada reported in [June 2026](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/news/2026/06/government-of-canada-continues-to-strengthen-access-to-oral-health-care.html) that more than 6.5 million people are covered.

## If you're not eligible: your other options

Most people who don't qualify have access to a dental plan or earn $90,000 or more. Here's where to look next.

- **You declined a workplace plan.** It already rules you out, so ask your employer when you can enrol.
- **You earn $90,000 or more, or you're self-employed.** An individual [health and dental insurance](/health-dental-insurance/) plan can cover dental, drugs, vision and paramedical care. Self-employed people should also look at [disability insurance for the self-employed](/disability-insurance/self-employed/).
- **You run a business with staff.** A [group benefits plan](/group-benefits/) can cover your team. Employees with access to it can't use the CDCP.

## Michael's take

As a planner, I'd settle the CDCP question before you buy any private plan that includes dental. Buying first can close the door, because a plan you own counts as access. If your family income sits under $90,000 and nobody in the household has access to a dental plan, apply first. Then look at a health-only plan for the gaps.

My rule of thumb is to treat the CDCP like any other income-tested benefit: it depends on filing taxes on time, every year. A missed return or a missed renewal leaves a gap that isn't paid back later. And if your employer offers dental and you opted out to save money, the CDCP isn't your backup. Take another look at the workplace plan before you decide to go without.
